[41.9] What is insurance?
[43.1] Well, insurance is a financial vehicle that helps spread risk.
[46.1] By taking a risk from an individual, and spreading that risk around a community, the individual
[50.9] is able to go about their personal or business life without crumbling from financial ruin.
[56.0] In the simplest terms, let’s look at two people.
[58.8] One is named Bob and the other Jim.
[60.6] Bob says to Jim, I’ll give you ten dollars, but if I lose my cell phone, you’ll have
[65.3] to buy me a new one.
[66.4] If Jim agrees, then that’s insurance right there.
[70.0] Insurance companies make money because they evaluate the risk and decide whether it is
[73.5] worth the gamble.
[74.5] Jim believes that Bob probably won’t lose his phone and he’ll therefore be ten dollars
[79.5] richer.
[80.5] If Jim finds 100 more people who are willing to give him 10 bucks each to cover their phones,
[85.0] he has 1,000 dollars.
[86.8] If one of those 100 people loses their phone and Jim pays 100 dollars as compensation,
[92.2] he still has 900 bucks.
[93.8] This insurance idea has been floating around since the ancient Chinese and the Babylonians
[98.1] spread their shipping risks.
[100.2] But it wasn’t until around the 17th century in London that modern insurance really took
[104.7] off.
[105.7] Merchant marine men and traders often hung out in coffee shops in the business district
[109.4] of London, and while drinking copious amounts of coffee, the idea of modern day insurance
[114.1] was born.
[115.1] Lloyds of London, the heart of worldwide insurance, was developed inside one of these coffee houses
[120.3] and here’s how it worked.
[121.7] First, you have the client.
[123.2] Say the client has a ship that he is nervous about losing to pirates offshore, or perhaps
[127.6] the vessel will be destroyed in bad weather.
[129.9] The client approaches an insurance broker.
[132.1] The broker looks at the ship, or pays someone to look at the ship, and they decide how much
[136.2] the total value of that ship is worth.
[138.5] The broker then assesses the risk.
[141.0] He asks the client where he is traveling to and what cargo he will be carrying.
[145.3] With all this information, he draws up an insurance policy which he shows to the third
[149.5] person in the chain - the underwriter.
[151.6] For a cheaper premium, the underwriter may exclude a few risks.
[155.0] And for a few more bucks, he may include some extra risks.
[158.7] Now there are normally lots of underwriters approached, but one will be the lead, and
[162.6] the lead underwriter, like Jim, will normally take the largest proportion of the risk and
[167.7] sign his name first on the policy document.
[170.5] He is known as the underwriter, as he writers his name under the risk on the insurance policy.
[175.4] The lead underwriter makes the major decisions when it comes to accepting the policy, and
[179.7] will be the main man to agree to any claims on the policy.
[183.2] Once the terms of the policy are agreed to, it is made legal, and the client is happy
[187.2] and the ship sets sail - but not before paying the insurance premium to the broker, who will
[191.8] take about 10%, and pass the rest on to the underwriter.
[195.5] But what should happen if pirates board the ship, steal the cargo, and burn it at sea?
[199.8] Well, the client (if he is still alive, if not, a representative of the client) will
[204.3] speak to the insurance broker and the broker will visit with the lead underwriter and tell
[208.3] him the bad news.
[209.7] The rest of the underwriters (there may well be as many as 20 on a big policy) are told
[213.9] the news and then the broker must negotiate the best claim settlement for the client or
[217.9] his or her representatives.
[219.7] The underwriters pay the money to the broker, who passes it on to the client, without deducting
[224.5] any cut.
[225.5] The broker makes his money once the premium is paid, and will help negotiate the best
[229.4] claims for his clients through gentlemanly honor and the prospect of future business.
[233.7] Now it may not be all bad news for the Underwriter.
[236.0] If he is wise and not greedy, he may have reinsured the policy.
[240.7] Reinsurance puts the underwriter in the position of the client.
[243.6] The underwriter sells the policy onto another underwriter or firm of underwriters, while
[248.4] retaining a share of the premium.
[250.5] Confused yet?
[251.8] Think back to Jim and his phone insurance.
[253.7] If Jim resold his 10 dollar phone policy for 9 dollars, rather than the 10 he received,
[259.2] then he gets to keep a dollar each for each of his 100 clients, meaning he has 100 dollars
[264.2] completely risk free.
[265.9] Similarly, much of the modern day insurance that flows through Lloyds of London is reinsured
[270.2] out of the building to smaller insurance companies all across the world.
[274.1] So what starts as a simple agreement between the client and the broker (or Jim and Bob)
[278.4] is spread across a business community who each stand to profit from the premium or take
[282.9] a cut of any losses.
[284.3] This is how insurance works – by the spreading of risk over communities.
[288.5] So that is how maritime insurance was born.
[290.5] It was developed through the need of ship-owners to carry on in business should they lose everything
[294.8] whilst at sea.
